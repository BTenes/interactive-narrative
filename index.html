From Shelter to Home: A Story of Dog Adoption in America IMT 561 Data Visualization: Design and Development
IMT 561 Final Project
Many people dream about having a dog, but not every dog starts life in a home. Across the U.S., thousands of dogs enter shelters each year. Some are quickly adopted, while others wait for a long time or leave the shelter through other outcomes.

Adoption is not just a number. Behind every intake, outcome, and breed label, there is a dog that might need a second chance.

Through these visualizations, we explore what happens to them after entering the shelter, which breeds appear more often, and how personality and care needs can help people make better adoption decisions.

Our goal is to encourage people to consider adoption and to choose a dog not only by appearance, but also by lifestyle fit, responsibility, and long-term care.

Whether you're considering adopting, volunteering, or simply want to understand the scale of the problem, this story is for you.
Where Are the Dogs?
Stray and community dogs don't follow state lines, but the resources to help them do.

Some states with smaller populations report surprisingly high intake numbers, suggesting that geography, climate, and local policy shape the problem as much as population size does. A dog on the streets of New Mexico faces a very different system than one in New York.

In the sections that follow, we zoom into Austin, Texas one of the most comprehensively documented shelter systems in the country. Austin's open data gives us a rare, detailed look at what happens to dogs once they enter the system: who gets adopted, who waits, and who doesn't make it out.

Not Every Dog Leaves the Same Way
Over ten years, more than 90,000 dogs passed through Austin Animal Center. Half were adopted. That sounds like a success and in many ways it is. Austin has long held "No Kill" status, meaning at least 90% of animals leave alive.

But the other half tells a more complicated story. Nearly a quarter were returned to their owners, it is strays reunited, not rehomed. Another fifth were transferred to rescue organizations, which absorb overflow when the shelter runs out of space or time. A small but real fraction (over 2%) were euthanized.

The "No Kill" label is earned, but it depends on a network of rescues, fosters, and volunteers working quietly behind the scenes. Without them, those numbers would look very different.
Austin's Shelter, by the Numbers
For most of the 2010s, Austin's Animal Center took in roughly 10,000 dogs a year. Adoptions stayed steady too, around 4,500 annually. The gap between the two lines was large, but predictable. Shelter workers knew what to expect.

Then 2020 hit. Intake collapsed as the city locked down and fewer stray dogs were reported. At the same time, a wave of first-time pet owners flooded adoption applications which is the so-called "pandemic puppy" effect. For one brief moment, Austin's shelter nearly closed the gap.

By 2022, the pattern reversed again. Many pandemic-era adopters returned their dogs as life returned to normal. Intake stayed lower than pre-pandemic levels, but adoptions fell too and haven't fully recovered since.
The Breed Nobody Wants to Talk About
Pit Bulls enter Austin's shelter at nearly twice the rate of any other breed. They also leave at a fraction of that rate. The gap between their intake and adoption bars is the widest on this entire chart, which is a visual representation of a problem that rescue workers have understood for years.

Breed stigma is real, and it shows up in the data. Labrador Retrievers and Chihuahuas also arrive in large numbers, but their adoption rates are significantly higher. For many breeds, the shelter is a temporary stop. For Pit Bulls, it too often becomes a long-term residence.

No single visualization can fix that. But seeing it clearly is a place to start.
Beyond the Numbers: Getting to Know the Dogs Behind the Statistics
Numbers can reveal patterns, but they rarely capture personality. To better understand the dogs behind these intake records, we looked beyond shelter data and explored breed characteristics commonly associated with some of the most frequently surrendered breeds. Traits such as trainability, energy level, grooming needs, and temperament provide a more complete picture of what living with these dogs is actually like.

By exploring breed profiles, adopters can move beyond reputation and discover traits that often make shelter dogs loyal, trainable, and rewarding companions. Understanding these characteristics helps bridge the gap between perception and reality which is an important step toward giving more shelter dogs a second chance.
Timing Matters More Than You'd Think
Dog adoptions don't happen evenly across the year. Summer brings a surge families with kids home from school, more time for walks, more energy for a new pet. The data bears this out clearly.

What's less obvious: the slow months. January and February are the quietest time of year for adoptions. Shelters are full from holiday surrenders, but foot traffic drops. Dogs that arrive in December may wait weeks before someone comes through the door.

If you're thinking about adopting, that's the moment when your choice matters most.
